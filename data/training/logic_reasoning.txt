Logic is the study of valid reasoning and relationships between statements.
Reasoning uses information to reach a conclusion, decision, explanation, or prediction.
A premise is a statement used as support for a conclusion.
A conclusion is a statement reached from premises or other information.
An argument is a set of statements in which some statements are offered as reasons for another statement.
A deductive argument aims to make its conclusion follow necessarily from its premises.
An inductive argument uses observations or evidence to support a conclusion that is probable rather than guaranteed.
An inference is a conclusion drawn from available information.
A valid deductive argument has a structure in which true premises cannot lead to a false conclusion.
A sound deductive argument is valid and has true premises.
An invalid argument can have true premises and still have a false conclusion.
An inductive argument can be strong or weak depending on how well its evidence supports its conclusion.
A conditional statement has an if part and a then part.
In a conditional statement, the antecedent is the condition and the consequent is the result.
The statement if A then B means that A is sufficient for B and B is necessary for A.
A biconditional states that two conditions are equivalent and can be written as A if and only if B.
A conjunction combines statements with and.
A disjunction combines statements with or.
A negation reverses the truth value of a statement.
A truth table lists possible truth values for logical statements and their combinations.
In classical two-valued logic, a proposition is either true or false.
The law of noncontradiction says a proposition cannot be both true and false in the same respect at the same time within classical logic.
The law of excluded middle states that a proposition or its negation is true within classical two-valued logic.
Modus ponens is a valid inference form: if A implies B, and A is true, then B follows.
Modus tollens is a valid inference form: if A implies B, and B is false, then A must be false.
Affirming the consequent is an invalid inference: from if A then B and B, one cannot conclude A.
Denying the antecedent is an invalid inference: from if A then B and not A, one cannot conclude not B.
A syllogism is a structured argument involving premises and a conclusion.
A categorical statement makes a claim about a class or category.
A counterexample is an example that disproves a universal claim.
A proof establishes a mathematical or logical statement from accepted assumptions and valid reasoning.
A proof by contradiction assumes the opposite of a desired conclusion and derives a contradiction.
Proof by induction establishes a statement for a sequence of cases using a base case and an inductive step.
A necessary condition must be present for something to occur.
A sufficient condition is enough to guarantee something under the stated rules.
Correlation means variables change together in some pattern.
Correlation alone does not establish causation.
Causation means a change in one factor contributes to a change in another under a specified causal relationship.
A confounding variable can influence both variables being compared and make a relationship misleading.
An assumption is something accepted temporarily or implicitly for the purpose of reasoning.
Hidden assumptions can make an argument weaker if they are unsupported.
An ambiguity occurs when a statement has multiple plausible interpretations.
A contradiction occurs when statements cannot all be true under the same interpretation.
Consistency means statements or observations do not conflict within a specified system.
An analogy can help explain a relationship but does not prove that two situations are identical.
An appeal to popularity treats popularity as evidence of truth without sufficient justification.
An appeal to authority can be reasonable when the authority is relevant and reliable, but authority alone does not guarantee a claim.
A straw man changes or weakens another person's position and then attacks the altered version.
A false dilemma presents too few options when more possibilities exist.
Circular reasoning uses a conclusion as support for itself.
A hasty generalization draws a broad conclusion from insufficient evidence.
Cherry-picking selects evidence while ignoring relevant evidence that points another way.
Confirmation bias is the tendency to favor information that supports existing beliefs.
A good reasoner actively checks for evidence that could challenge a preferred conclusion.
Evidence should be evaluated for relevance, reliability, quantity, quality, and context.
A claim should be separated from the evidence offered to support it.
A conclusion should not be more certain than the evidence justifies.
Uncertainty is a normal part of reasoning when information is incomplete.
Probability can represent uncertainty about events or hypotheses.
Expected value combines possible outcomes with their probabilities in a mathematical model.
Bayesian reasoning updates the relative plausibility of hypotheses using evidence and prior information.
A prior represents beliefs or probabilities before considering new evidence.
A likelihood describes how compatible observed evidence is with a hypothesis under a model.
A posterior represents an updated probability after evidence is considered.
A decision can be rationally evaluated by comparing possible outcomes, probabilities, goals, and constraints.
Optimization means seeking the best result according to a defined objective and constraints.
A trade-off occurs when improving one objective makes another objective harder to improve.
A constraint limits which solutions are possible.
Decomposition breaks a complex problem into smaller subproblems.
Abstraction removes unnecessary details while keeping information relevant to the task.
Pattern recognition identifies repeated structures or relationships.
Algorithmic reasoning describes a problem-solving process as ordered steps.
A heuristic is a practical rule or strategy that can find useful solutions without guaranteeing the best possible result.
A brute-force method checks many or all possible candidates when that is feasible.
A greedy algorithm repeatedly chooses an option that appears best at the current step.
A greedy choice is not always globally optimal.
A search problem asks how to find a desired state or solution among possibilities.
A state represents the relevant condition of a system at a particular point.
A goal describes a desired state or outcome.
A plan is a sequence of actions intended to reach a goal.
Backtracking explores choices and reverses a choice when it leads to an invalid or unsuccessful path.
Debugging is a form of reasoning in which observations and tests are used to identify causes of unexpected behavior.
A hypothesis is a proposed explanation that can be tested against evidence.
An experiment changes or observes variables in a controlled way to test a hypothesis.
A prediction is an expected observation that follows from a hypothesis or model.
Reproducibility means that a result can be obtained again using the specified method and conditions.
A model is a simplified representation of a system.
Models are useful because they focus on selected relationships rather than representing every detail.
A model can be useful while still being incomplete.
Good reasoning distinguishes observations from interpretations.
An observation is information obtained through measurement or direct sensing.
An interpretation explains what an observation might mean.
A conclusion should account for alternative explanations when they are plausible.
If two explanations make different predictions, observations can help distinguish between them.
Reasoning from examples can suggest a pattern, but more evidence may be needed before treating it as a general rule.
