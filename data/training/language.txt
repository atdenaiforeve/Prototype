Language is a system humans use to communicate meaning through sounds, signs, symbols, or writing.
A language can communicate facts, questions, requests, feelings, ideas, stories, and relationships.
A speaker or writer chooses words and structures based on context, purpose, audience, and language conventions.
A listener or reader interprets language using words, grammar, context, background knowledge, and expectations.
A word is a unit of language that can carry meaning or perform a grammatical function.
A sentence is a grammatical unit that usually expresses a complete thought in standard written English.
A phrase is a group of words that functions together but does not necessarily contain a complete subject-predicate structure.
A clause contains a subject and a predicate and can be independent or dependent.
A subject is the person, thing, or idea that a clause is about or that performs an action in many sentence structures.
A predicate contains the verb and information about the subject.
A noun commonly names a person, place, thing, animal, or idea.
A pronoun can stand in for a noun or noun phrase.
A verb expresses an action, occurrence, or state.
An adjective modifies or describes a noun or pronoun.
An adverb can modify a verb, adjective, another adverb, or an entire clause.
A preposition expresses relationships such as location, direction, time, or connection.
A conjunction connects words, phrases, or clauses.
An interjection can express a sudden feeling or reaction.
A determiner helps specify a noun, as in words such as the, a, an, this, or some.
Grammar is the system of patterns and rules used to form and interpret expressions in a language.
Syntax concerns how words and phrases are arranged.
Semantics concerns meaning.
Pragmatics concerns how meaning depends on context and use.
Morphology studies the internal structure and formation of words.
Phonetics studies speech sounds as physical phenomena.
Phonology studies how sounds function within a particular language.
A morpheme is a meaningful unit in a word.
A prefix is a morpheme added to the beginning of a word.
A suffix is a morpheme added to the end of a word.
Inflection changes grammatical information without normally creating a completely new dictionary word, such as plural or tense marking.
Derivation can create a new word or change its grammatical category.
Singular commonly refers to one.
Plural commonly refers to more than one.
Tense helps locate an event in time, with English commonly using past, present, and future constructions.
Aspect describes how an event unfolds, such as whether it is ongoing or completed.
Voice can describe whether a clause is active or passive.
An active sentence often places the agent before the verb.
A passive sentence often places the affected participant before a form of be plus a past participle.
Subject-verb agreement means grammatical forms should agree in relevant features such as number in standard English.
Pronouns can have person, number, and case distinctions.
First person refers to the speaker or writer.
Second person refers to the person or people being addressed.
Third person refers to other people, things, or ideas.
A declarative sentence normally makes a statement.
An interrogative sentence asks a question.
An imperative sentence gives a command or instruction.
An exclamatory sentence expresses strong emotion in a conventional grammatical form.
Punctuation helps readers interpret written language.
A period commonly marks the end of a declarative sentence.
A question mark commonly marks a direct question.
An exclamation mark can indicate emphasis or strong emotion.
A comma can separate items, phrases, clauses, or introductory material depending on sentence structure.
An apostrophe can mark possession or omitted letters in contractions.
Quotation marks can indicate direct quotations or certain titles depending on a style guide.
Capitalization marks the beginning of sentences and proper names and follows language conventions.
A paragraph is a group of related sentences organized around a central idea or purpose.
A topic sentence often introduces the main idea of a paragraph.
Coherence means ideas connect in a way that is understandable.
Cohesion means linguistic devices help connect parts of a text.
A text can have a purpose such as informing, explaining, persuading, entertaining, instructing, or telling a story.
A fact is a claim that can in principle be checked against evidence.
An opinion expresses a belief, judgment, preference, or evaluation.
A claim is a statement presented as true or proposed for consideration.
Evidence is information used to support or evaluate a claim.
A source is something that provides information or evidence.
A reliable explanation should distinguish known facts from uncertainty or speculation.
A definition states the meaning or scope of a term.
An example demonstrates how a concept can work in a particular case.
An analogy compares two things to help explain a relationship, but an analogy is not automatically proof that the things are identical.
A summary states the main ideas of a text without reproducing every detail.
A paraphrase restates an idea in different wording while preserving its meaning.
A quotation reproduces another speaker's or writer's exact words and should be attributed appropriately.
A narrative tells events or describes experiences, often involving characters, setting, sequence, and change.
A character is a person, animal, or other entity that participates in a story.
A setting describes where and when a story takes place.
A plot is the sequence and relationship of events in a narrative.
Dialogue represents interaction between speakers.
Tone is the attitude or emotional quality conveyed by language.
Register is the style of language appropriate to a particular situation or audience.
Formal language is often used in academic, professional, or official contexts.
Informal language is common in casual conversation and friendly communication.
Slang is highly informal vocabulary associated with particular groups, places, or contexts.
Idioms are expressions whose conventional meaning cannot always be inferred directly from their individual words.
Metaphor describes one thing in terms of another to create an implied comparison.
Simile explicitly compares things using words such as like or as.
Personification gives human characteristics to nonhuman things in figurative language.
Hyperbole is deliberate exaggeration used for emphasis or effect.
Irony involves a contrast between expected and actual meaning or circumstances; its precise type depends on context.
Synonyms are words with similar meanings, although they are rarely identical in every context.
Antonyms are words with contrasting meanings.
Homophones sound alike but have different meanings or spellings.
Polysemy occurs when one word has multiple related meanings.
Context can help resolve ambiguity when a word or sentence has more than one possible interpretation.
A question is not always asking for a factual answer; it may request clarification, reasoning, action, or opinion.
A good response should address what the speaker actually asked rather than merely repeating nearby words.
Conversation includes turn-taking, responding to previous statements, asking questions, clarifying misunderstandings, and maintaining a topic.
A conversational response can acknowledge a statement before adding new information.
Clarification is useful when a message is genuinely ambiguous and the missing information matters.
An instruction tells someone or something to perform a task.
Instructions can include goals, constraints, inputs, steps, and expected outputs.
A conditional instruction applies only when a specified condition is met.
A sequence describes actions in an intended order.
A specification describes required behavior or properties of a system.
A programming language uses formal syntax and semantics, while natural language is generally more flexible and context-dependent.
Tokenization divides text into units used by a language-processing system.
A token can represent a word, part of a word, punctuation, or another text unit depending on the tokenizer.
A vocabulary is a set of tokens known by a language model or language-processing system.
Language models estimate patterns or probabilities over sequences of tokens.
Next-token prediction asks which token is likely to come after a given context.
Grammar and meaning can both be learned statistically from examples, but statistical learning can also produce mistakes.
Training examples should contain varied, accurate, and useful language.
A language model should not treat every generated statement as automatically true.
When information is uncertain, a system should communicate uncertainty rather than inventing confidence.
Good communication aims to be understandable, relevant, accurate, and appropriate to the context.
